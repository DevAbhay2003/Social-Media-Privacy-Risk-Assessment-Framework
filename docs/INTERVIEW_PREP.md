# Top 10 Cybersecurity & Privacy Interview Questions & Model Answers

### Question 1: Explain your project.
**Answer:**
"I built the **Social Media Privacy Risk Assessment Framework**, a defensive cybersecurity web application that evaluates an individual's or employee's social media exposure, account security habits, and digital footprint without scraping or harvesting real accounts. 

The user completes a structured 44-question assessment across 10 defense-in-depth domains—such as profile visibility, contact disclosure, real-time location sharing, post audiences, tagging permissions, MFA, and third-party apps. The framework normalizes these inputs into category risk scores from 0 to 100 and computes an overall weighted privacy risk score, categorizing the exposure into Low, Moderate, High, or Critical. 

It generates prioritized, actionable remediation steps, a downloadable PDF audit report, and features an interactive **Privacy Improvement Simulator** showing the mathematical score reduction when hardening specific settings. The platform was built with Flask, SQLite, and Chart.js, strictly adheres to Privacy by Design, and stores zero raw PII."

### Question 2: What is the technical difference between Privacy and Security?
**Answer:**
"Privacy and security are interconnected but fundamentally distinct concepts. **Security** focuses on confidentiality, integrity, and availability—protecting systems, accounts, and credentials against unauthorized access, compromise, or abuse. **Privacy**, on the other hand, governs how personal information is collected, exposed, shared, and utilized.

A concrete example illustrates this distinction: A user might have a 30-character random password, an authenticator app with MFA, and biometric lock enabled. Their account security is exceptionally strong. However, if that user publicly posts their personal phone number, full birth date, employer badge, and daily 7 AM running route, their privacy is severely compromised. Therefore, strong account security does not equate to strong privacy."

### Question 3: What is a digital footprint, and how does your project measure it?
**Answer:**
"A digital footprint is the persistent trail of data and records generated by a user's activities on digital services. We categorize it into an **Active Footprint**—content intentionally shared such as posts, photos, tagged locations, and bio descriptions—and a **Passive Footprint**, which includes metadata, search indexing, and third-party tracker trails.

In this project, rather than spying on users or scraping live accounts, we evaluate digital footprints through defensible self-reported hygiene metrics. Specifically, Category J evaluates whether users audit and archive historical timeline posts older than 12 months, whether they maintain abandoned zombie accounts, whether they conduct defensive self-OSINT searches, and how frequently they execute platform privacy checkups."

### Question 4: How does your privacy-risk scoring engine work mathematically?
**Answer:**
"The scoring engine operates on a two-tier weighted rubric. First, user responses within each of the 10 categories are converted into numerical risk points based on evaluated risk. Each category is normalized to a 0–100 scale, where 0 represents minimal assessed exposure and 100 represents maximum vulnerability.

Second, the overall risk score is calculated as the weighted sum of all category scores divided by total weights:
$$\text{Overall Score} = \sum (\text{Category Score}_i \times \text{Weight}_i)$$
Category weights are configured based on threat severity—for example, Personal Information Exposure (15%), Location Privacy (15%), and Account Security (15%) carry higher weights than Tagging (5%) or Digital Footprint (5%). The resulting score maps deterministically to standard tiers: 0–20 is Low, 21–40 is Moderate, 41–70 is High, and 71–100 is Critical."

### Question 5: How does social-media oversharing directly increase social-engineering risk?
**Answer:**
"Threat actors utilize Open Source Intelligence (OSINT) from public social media profiles to transition generic spam into high-trust spear-phishing and pretexting attacks. When an attacker can accurately reference a target's employer, job role, direct manager, alma mater, or recent vacation plans, the victim's cognitive skepticism decreases significantly.

For example, if an attacker observes an employee celebrating a promotion at Acme Corp and posting a photo of their badge, they can craft a convincing message posing as Acme IT Helpdesk requesting urgent single-sign-on verification. My framework evaluates these oversharing vectors in Category B, C, and I, educating users to decouple public identity from private authentication secrets."

### Question 6: Why did you include MFA and account security in a privacy assessment project?
**Answer:**
"Because privacy and account security intersect at the point of account compromise. While strong security doesn't guarantee privacy, a security breach immediately destroys privacy. If an adversary compromises a social media account through credential stuffing or password spraying, they gain access to non-public direct messages, private photos, contact lists, and private phone numbers.

MFA acts as a critical defensive barrier that halts up to 99% of bulk automated credential attacks. In my framework, Category G evaluates whether MFA is enabled, whether the user uses modern TOTP authenticator apps or hardware keys instead of vulnerable SMS, and whether unrecognized login alerts are active."

### Question 7: What privacy risks are created by third-party application integrations (OAuth)?
**Answer:**
"When users click 'Sign in with Facebook' or connect interactive quizzes, games, and third-party tools, they grant OAuth 2.0 authorization tokens. These tokens often authorize scopes allowing the third-party application to read user profiles, access friends lists, and inspect email addresses.

Over time, users accumulate dozens of forgotten integrations. If any of those third-party app developers suffer a data breach or are acquired by unscrupulous marketing firms, the user's data is exposed through the software supply chain. My framework enforces the **Principle of Least Privilege**, asking whether users audit and revoke dormant third-party integrations biannually."

### Question 8: What is Privacy by Design, and how did you apply it in your code?
**Answer:**
"Privacy by Design means embedding data protection and privacy directly into the architecture and operational defaults of a software system, rather than attempting to bolt it on after development. 

In this framework, I applied:
1. **Data Minimization:** We never collect actual phone numbers, passwords, emails, home addresses, or coordinates. We only ask configuration questions (*'Is your phone number visible?'*).
2. **In-Memory Media Processing:** The EXIF metadata inspector processes images entirely in RAM and never writes uploaded pictures to disk.
3. **Database Minimization:** The SQLite database schema stores only assessment IDs, scores, and finding categories.
4. **User Control (Right to Erasure):** A user can click 'Delete Record' or call `DELETE /api/assessment/{id}` to permanently purge their assessment record, complying with GDPR Article 17."

### Question 9: What is the Privacy Improvement Simulator, and how does it benefit users?
**Answer:**
"The simulator addresses the psychological barrier in cybersecurity where users feel overwhelmed by security advice. Instead of presenting static warnings, the simulator allows users to toggle 14 specific settings—like making their phone private, disabling real-time location sharing, turning on MFA, and enabling tag review.

The backend dynamically recalculates the category scores and overall risk score, displaying a clear before-and-after comparison—for example, reducing an assessed score from 72 (Critical) down to 34 (Moderate), representing a 52.8% reduction in assessed attack surface. It visualizes the impact on a real-time delta chart, providing immediate behavioral positive reinforcement."

### Question 10: How did you test your framework, and how would you expand it in the future?
**Answer:**
"I built a comprehensive automated test suite consisting of 34 tests using `pytest`. The suite tests:
- Boundary conditions (0, 20, 40, 70, 100 score cutoffs).
- Extremes: Fully private profiles versus fully public profiles.
- Specific risk vectors: Public phone, missing MFA, live check-ins, travel plans, and badge photos.
- Data minimization verification: Inspecting database tables to verify zero columns contain sensitive PII.
- In-memory EXIF extraction and binary header stripping.
- Full REST API lifecycle including submission and deletion.

In the future, I would expand the framework by adding platform-specific guided privacy checkup scripts for Instagram, LinkedIn, and X, implementing organizational policy benchmarking for corporate security awareness training, and adding an entirely client-side WebAssembly mode for completely offline assessment execution."
